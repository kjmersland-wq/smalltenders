Working across Europe

Practical notes for small firms bidding outside their home country.

Please read first. This page is general information, not legal, tax or employment advice. Rules change, and they differ by trade, employer and contract. Always check with the official authority in the country where the work is done, and read the tender documents. If something here is out of date, tell us. Written October 2026.

Public tenders work a little differently in every country, and a few practical things decide whether a job is realistic for you: working hours, pay rules, what you must report before you start, and language. These are honest rules of thumb, not guarantees.

The basics that apply almost everywhere in the EU and EEA

Working hours
EU law sets minimum standards: no more than 48 hours a week on average, at least 11 hours of rest a day and at least four weeks of paid annual leave. National law and collective agreements can be stricter.
Posted workers
If you send your own employees to work in another country for a contract, they are posted workers. While they are there, the host country’s rules on pay, working time, rest, holidays and health and safety apply to them.
Before you start
Many countries ask you to register or notify before work begins. Carry the A1 certificate, which shows which country’s social security applies, and the employment contracts.
Pay
Pay comes from a statutory minimum wage, from collective agreements, or from both. For posted workers, the legally binding pay terms of the host country for that trade apply, often including allowances.

Country and region notes

The Nordics

Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Finland, Iceland

Working hours
Full time is typically around 37 to 40 hours a week. Collective agreements often set the details for overtime and breaks.
Pay and agreements
None of the Nordic countries has a general statutory minimum wage. Pay is set by collective agreements, and in some Norwegian trades (such as construction, electrical work and cleaning) those agreements are extended to every firm working there, foreign ones included.
Posting rules
Expect to register or notify the authorities before you start. Denmark’s RUT register is a well-known example. Plan to follow local collective-agreement pay for your trade.
Language
Tender documents are in the local language. On site, English is often fine.

Poland

Poland

Working hours
The standard is 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week on average. Overtime rules are strict.
Pay and agreements
Poland has a statutory minimum wage that is set each year. Sector collective agreements are less common than in the Nordics.
Posting rules
Foreign firms are generally expected to notify the authorities about posted workers and to follow Polish pay and working-time rules.
Language
The local language matters, both for the documents and for the work on site. Plan on Polish, or work with someone who speaks it.

Germany

Germany

Working hours
Up to 8 hours a day, extendable to 10 within limits, and 48 hours a week at most. Sunday work is restricted.
Pay and agreements
Germany has a statutory minimum wage. Several trades, including construction, roofing, electrical work, painting and cleaning, have their own higher minimum wages that also apply to foreign firms.
Posting rules
In certain sectors foreign firms must notify the customs authority before work starts and keep records of hours and pay ready for inspections.
Language
The local language matters, both for the documents and for the work on site. Plan on German, or work with someone who speaks it.

France

France

Working hours
The legal working week is 35 hours. Hours above that count as overtime.
Pay and agreements
France has a statutory minimum wage. Sector collective agreements often add higher pay scales.
Posting rules
Foreign firms declare posted workers online before they start (the SIPSI declaration). Building workers also need a professional card, the carte BTP.
Language
The local language is strongly preferred, in the documents and in day-to-day work. Expect to need it for your bid as well as on site.

Spain

Spain

Working hours
The legal maximum is 40 hours a week on average, with daily and weekly rest set by law.
Pay and agreements
Spain has a statutory minimum wage. Sector and provincial collective agreements often set higher pay for each trade.
Posting rules
Foreign firms notify the authorities before posting workers and follow Spanish pay and working-time rules.
Language
The local language is strongly preferred, in the documents and in day-to-day work. Expect to need it for your bid as well as on site.

Italy

Italy

Working hours
Normal working time is 40 hours a week, and national collective agreements set the details.
Pay and agreements
Italy has no general statutory minimum wage. National collective agreements for each sector set the benchmark in practice.
Posting rules
Foreign firms are expected to notify the Ministry of Labour before posting workers and to apply the pay of the sector’s national agreement.
Language
The local language is strongly preferred, in the documents and in day-to-day work. Expect to need it for your bid as well as on site.

The Netherlands

Netherlands

Working hours
Dutch law caps hours per shift and per week. Many collective agreements (CAOs) set shorter normal weeks.
Pay and agreements
There is a statutory minimum wage. Many trades have CAOs that apply to everyone in that sector, including foreign firms.
Posting rules
Foreign firms report posted workers to the Dutch authorities before work starts and follow CAO pay where it applies.
Language
English is widely understood, but the tender documents are in Dutch.

The Baltics

Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania

Working hours
Normal full time is 40 hours a week, in line with EU rules.
Pay and agreements
Each country has a statutory minimum wage. Collective agreements are less widespread than in the Nordics.
Posting rules
Foreign firms usually notify the labour inspectorate before posting workers and follow local pay and working-time rules.
Language
English is relatively common, especially in business. The documents are normally in the local language.
